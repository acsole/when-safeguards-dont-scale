# Safeguards Case Study
### Reading a real single-user AI-assisted project through the lens of AI Safeguards — and projecting it to adversarial scale

> **Status:** living document. Sections are drafted via a multi-agent pipeline (Haiku drafts → Sonnet quality-guardian audits → Opus + author approve). Section order: S0 → S1 → S3 → S4 → S2 → S5 → S6 → S7. S0 is revisited after S5.
> **Scope note:** this is an analytical case study, not a claim of production-grade security. Limitations are stated explicitly in §S6.

---

## Elevator Pitch

Safeguards that protect a single-user application—human approval gates, automated checks, audit trails—may degrade or fail when scaled to adversarial populations. This case study examines a real AI-assisted project built with such mechanisms from inception: deny-by-default governance, quality checkpoints across agent workflows, and traceability hooks that caught policy violations in supervised operation. We trace each safeguard from single-founder oversight to population-scale pressure, exposing both the principles that hold under adversarial stress and the architectural fractures that emerge only in production. The central question is whether the mechanisms designed for one trusted supervisor can survive when millions of users, thousands of concurrent agents, and bad actors all probe the same checkpoints simultaneously.

## Context: The System Under Study

### The Vitalis Project

Vitalis (formerly CalmKit) is a single-founder software initiative launched by Andrés with zero capital. Its goal is to build a family of free Progressive Web Applications (PWAs) that address wellness challenges—anxiety, sleep, burnout, digestion—initially targeting organic growth via search engine optimization and monetization through Google AdSense and affiliate marketing. The technical stack is deliberately constrained: vanilla HTML5, CSS3, and ES6+ JavaScript with no external frameworks, Service Workers for offline functionality, browser-native storage (localStorage), and free hosting via Netlify or Vercel. The ambition is to scale from a solo founder to a sustainable business serving millions of users without venture capital, leveraging AI assistance to multiply productivity.

The system's safeguard relevance stems from its wellness domain. Tools generated by Vitalis handle mental health and physical wellbeing content—areas where erroneous advice or unsupervised AI output could harm users. Vitalis is explicitly built on the principle that such tools require comprehensive disclaimers, legal review, and human approval before reaching any audience. The project's governing charter emphasizes a core commitment: to honor life by caring for it and protecting it without causing harm, and to ensure that any tool which could be misread as substitute medical or psychological advice carries all legally required disclaimers, with Andrés consulting and approving the form before publication.

### The Multi-Agent Working Method

Rather than rely on a single AI model, Vitalis adopts a collaborative division-of-labor approach across multiple large language model instances. The method assigns distinct roles to models suited to each role: **Opus** acts as project director and final evaluator, synthesizing proposals and architectural decisions; **Sonnet** serves as the quality guardian, reviewing work for consistency, accuracy, and alignment with constraints; **Haiku** drafts atomic, modular pieces of work—"fichas de encargo" (work orders)—each with explicit boundaries of what must be included and what must be avoided.

Critically, this is not autonomous multi-agent reasoning. Every output is subject to human approval. Andrés reviews all substantive decisions before any code is written, deployed, or published. A proposal must pass human inspection and receive explicit approval to proceed. Between sessions, a persistent file-based memory system (git-tracked markdown files) preserves prior decisions, constraints, architectural choices, and lessons learned, ensuring continuity across separate conversations.

### The ASLAN-ARC Dashboard

In parallel, Andrés maintains ASLAN-ARC, a multi-project monitoring dashboard styled as an "Iron Man HUD"—a real-time browser-based view aggregating status and metrics across multiple concurrent projects. Built in vanilla JavaScript with Web Components (no dependencies), the dashboard enforces two non-negotiable principles: **data honesty** (metrics and state reflect reality; no fabrication of data to show false progress) and **automated enforcement**. An audit hook inspects edits to the dashboard's core `.js` files and blocks any edit that violates the project's `file://` architectural invariants (e.g., patterns involving `import`, `export`, `type="module"`, or `fetch()`); this hook has fired on real edits in practice. The dashboard maintains comprehensive telemetry and audit logging, creating a persistent record of what changed, when, and by whom—essential for reviewing whether safeguard boundaries held under operational stress.

### The User and What Is Protected

The "user" or supervisor in this system is singular: Andrés, the founder. What the safeguards protect includes: (1) **codebase integrity**—ensuring that code changes align with the project's architecture and constraints; (2) **human decision authority**—enforcing that no AI system unilaterally commits changes, makes strategic pivots, or publishes content without Andrés's explicit prior consent; and (3) **end-user wellbeing**—ensuring that any wellness tool reaching eventual users includes appropriate legal safeguards, medical disclaimers, and has undergone human review for potential to cause harm.

This is a constrained, supervised environment: one human, known governance boundaries, explicit approval gates, and persistent audit trails. The case study will examine how this structure—designed for a single founder and small AI-assisted team—scales (or fails to scale) when the system moves toward production with adversarial populations, untrusted operators, or distributed autonomous agents.

## The Four Layers

Defense in depth for scaling AI systems operates across four structural layers, each designed to catch different kinds of failures. The diagram below traces a single task through all four layers, showing how a malicious or erroneous directive is intercepted, contained, detected, and potentially recovered from. Vitalis demonstrates this architecture in practice:

**Prevention** — stop bad actions before they happen.
Bad directives never reach execution because governance is built into the problem statement itself. In Vitalis, the CLAUDE.md file codifies a deny-by-default governance rule: "The AI will not execute any code, deployment, or change without first presenting a clear plan and obtaining explicit approval from Andrés exclusively." [es. "La IA no ejecutará ningún código, despliegue o cambio sin antes exponer un plan claro y obtener aprobación explícita por parte de Andrés en exclusividad."] This is not a guideline; it is a structural constraint on what the system can attempt. Additionally, work is scoped via "fichas de encargo" (work orders)—each task specifies not only what must be done but explicitly what must NOT be touched. An example work order might specify that a section must contain role boundaries, must not touch git history, and must not be written to files or committed by the agent. The boundaries are unambiguous. An agent instructed with contradictory directives—write a section but also do not modify anything—faces a constraint it cannot satisfy, so it stays in the design phase rather than attempting to execute.

**Containment** — limit what a component can do, so a failure stays bounded.
Even if prevention fails, the blast radius must be limited. In Vitalis, the drafting agent (Haiku) is operationally limited to returning text—it has no permission to write files, commit to git, or deploy. If Haiku's output contains a serious error, the damage is confined to a chat message that the next layer can reject. The file system and git repository remain untouched. In contrast, an executor agent that could write and commit without review would mean a corrupted file is live until someone notices; with Haiku constrained to text-only, the error is visible before any persistence.

**Detection** — notice when something has gone wrong.
A second independent role, Sonnet, acts as a quality guardian. It audits each draft from Haiku against its ficha (its work order) before acceptance, checking field by field: Is the content there? Is the tone right? Are assumptions grounded? During the pilot run of Module 1 of the method documentation, Sonnet caught that the anchor to real projects was missing—a semantic flaw that neither Opus nor the human supervisor had noticed while reading quickly. Every change is logged as a separate git commit, making the history reviewable and the responsible party visible. A human can inspect what changed and decide whether to accept or revert.

**Recovery** — restore a good state after a failure.
Vitalis persists each accepted section as a distinct git commit, preserving full history and enabling reversion to any prior state. Persistent memory (stored in `.md` memory files linked to sessions) records decisions and resolved issues, so if a problem recurs in a later session, the solution is already documented and does not have to be rediscovered.

## Threat Model: The Single-User Baseline

At its current scale—a solo founder (Andrés) working with Claude across a single persistent session—Vitalis faces five concrete failure modes that could undermine the system's safeguards or harm the eventual end-users who interact with its tools. The defenses against these threats are the four layers described above; this section deliberately stays on the threats themselves.

**Model Hallucination & Fabrication.** Claude may generate plausible-sounding but false facts: invented file paths, nonexistent commit hashes, misquoted or truncated source material, or citations to evidence that does not exist. This has already manifested in the project's own authorship pipeline—sections have been drafted with fabricated quotes and distorted references that only surfaced during human review. A hallucination embedded in user-facing copy (e.g., a wellness tip framed as research-backed when it is not) could spread to real people without the founder noticing before publication.

**Constraint Drift.** Over the course of a long session or across multiple sessions, Claude may gradually depart from the agreed scope, conventions, or constraints—shifting the intended meaning of a work order, silently expanding what it believes it should do, or interpreting guardrails more loosely each time they are applied. The risk is not a single large violation but incremental loosening that erodes the deny-by-default posture until a once-clear boundary becomes fuzzy.

**Harm via Health-Adjacent Content.** Vitalis tools touch anxiety, sleep, burnout, and digestive wellness—domains where end-users might reasonably misinterpret guidance as medical or psychological advice. If Claude generates text that reads authoritatively about a health condition without appropriate disclaimers or hedging, a real person using the tool later could be misled or harmed. At single-user scale, the founder must catch every such instance before it ships; a failure in that human gate would allow harmful content to reach users.

**Loss of Human Gate.** The core rule is deny-by-default: Claude proposes, Andrés approves, then implementation happens. If Claude begins executing—committing code, deploying, or publishing—without the required approval step, the safeguard collapses. This can happen either as overt disobedience (a hypothetical *I decided to deploy*) or through reframing (a hypothetical *this is just a small edit, not really an action*) that lets the AI skip the approval gate entirely.

**Scope Leakage.** Work tasks and modules have defined boundaries. Claude may touch files, dependencies, or concerns it was not asked to address—for instance, drafting content for a different subsystem, modifying shared configuration without coordination, or conflating responsibilities across the multi-agent team (Haiku-as-drafter, Sonnet-as-reviewer, Opus-as-director). Each such bleed erodes clarity and introduces inconsistency that the human must detect and correct.

## Where It Breaks at Scale

Strip away the implementation detail and nearly every safeguard examined so far rests on the same scarce resource: the judgment of one trusted human. Deny-by-default works because a single person can review what a single agent proposes before anything is committed. Detection works because that same person — or someone equally accountable — can look at every diff before it lands. Containment works because a human, not the executor, decides what gets persisted. Recovery works because the history that human curates is short enough to read and linear enough to revert. None of these are properties of the algorithms; they are properties of having exactly one well-calibrated decision-maker in the loop, with enough time to look at everything that matters. That person is the keystone of the entire architecture, and scale is precisely the force that removes the keystone first.

Scale does something else, too, something easy to miss if the analysis stays mechanical: it changes the nature of the threat. In the single-user case examined throughout this document, every failure mode considered — hallucination, drift, an over-eager edit — is accidental. Nothing in the system is trying to defeat the safeguards; the worst case is a mistake that a careful human catches. At population scale, with an adversary capable of orchestrating many agents in parallel, the failure mode is no longer accidental. The adversary is not making mistakes; the adversary is running a search. Tens of thousands of agents probing a system are, functionally, an optimization process aimed at finding the seam between safeguards — the input the reviewer didn't sample, the approval queue depth that forces a rubber stamp, the edit that is individually compliant but jointly harmful. The question the architecture must answer changes from "how do we catch errors we didn't intend" to "how do we withstand pressure that is intended, sustained, and adaptive." A safeguard that comfortably absorbs accidents can still fail completely against optimization pressure, because accidents are unimodal and bounded while an adversarial search actively redistributes its effort toward whatever is weakest.

What follows works through the four layers in order of fragility — which one gives first, which one second — using a single adversary scenario threaded through all four so the failures can be seen compounding rather than considered in isolation.

**The scenario.** An adversary controls on the order of tens of thousands of automated agents and directs them at a system that nominally retains the same four-layer defense examined elsewhere in this case study: human approval at the front, independent review of outputs, an executor constrained to propose rather than persist, and a recoverable audit history. The adversary's goal is not to break any single layer outright but to find, across all four simultaneously, the path of least resistance.

**1. Human-in-the-loop / deny-by-default — the first to break.** The single-user version of this safeguard assumes that human attention is effectively O(1): one project, one founder, a trickle of proposals arriving slowly enough that each can be read, understood, and either approved or pushed back on before the next arrives. The assumption that fails at scale is not that humans get worse at judgment — it is that action volume becomes O(n) while review capacity stays roughly fixed. Tens of thousands of agents submitting requests in parallel produce an approval queue that grows far faster than any team of human reviewers can clear it. At that point the system has exactly three paths, all bad. It can delegate approval to an automated policy — at which point the adversary stops attacking the human and starts attacking the policy, probing it for the inputs it misclassifies, the edge cases its rules don't cover, the phrasing that slips past a filter built for different phrasing. It can let the human gate degrade in place, where overwhelmed approvers under throughput pressure stop actually reading and start clicking approve to keep the queue moving — deny-by-default still exists on paper, but it has become deny-by-default in name and approve-by-exhaustion in practice. Or, under enough business pressure to ship at speed, the gate is removed outright, formally or informally, because it has become the throughput bottleneck. In the running scenario, the adversary's flood of agents is aimed exactly here: not to get any single request approved, but to push the queue past the point where careful review is humanly possible, forcing one of these three degradations.

**2. Detection — independent review of every output.** This safeguard's single-user form assumes two things: that all output can be reviewed, and that the reviewer doing so is trustworthy. Both assumptions erode at scale, in different ways. Volume defeats the first: review-everything is only tractable when "everything" is small, and at population scale it is not, so detection shifts from exhaustive review to sampling. The adversary's task becomes characterizing the sampling window — through repeated, low-stakes probing, tens of thousands of agents can map which classes of input tend to draw scrutiny and which tend to pass through unexamined, then route the harmful payload through the latter. This is the direct continuation of the scenario: having pushed Prevention into a degraded state, the same agent population now spends part of its volume specifically learning what the reviewer doesn't look at, and the rest exploiting it. The second assumption — that the reviewer is trustworthy — also stops being free at scale. A single founder reviewing their own project has no principal-agent problem; a population-scale system requires many reviewers, drawn from a larger and less individually vetted pool, and now has an insider-threat surface that the single-user analog never had to account for at all.

**3. Containment — the executor returns text and cannot persist.** The single-user safeguard here assumes a human gate sits between proposal and persistence, so containment can be read as a restatement of Prevention's promise: nothing irreversible happens without the keystone human approving it. But Layer 1 has already broken in this scenario — the queue has forced delegation, degradation, or removal of that gate. Once it is gone, the executor can no longer wait for a human to act as the write barrier; something has to persist state directly, at the volume the system now operates at, or the system simply stops functioning. The blast radius of a single bad action changes correspondingly. In the single-user case the worst plausible outcome of a contained failure is a malformed chat response that never leaves the conversation. Once containment is removed to keep pace with volume, the same class of failure — one bad edit, individually unremarkable — now writes to production state that millions of people depend on. This is the part of the scenario where the adversary's agents stop trying to produce obviously harmful output (which Detection might still catch on the margin) and instead submit edits that each pass every automated invariant in isolation, while being harmful only in aggregate or in combination — a failure mode containment was never designed to catch, because containment was built to stop a single bad write, not to reason about the joint effect of ten thousand individually compliant ones.

**4. Recovery — git revert and a persistent, auditable history.** The single-user safeguard assumes a history that is slow-growing, linear, and small enough that a human can read it and choose the right point to revert to. At adversarial population scale this assumption fails on two fronts at once. First, with many concurrent agents committing simultaneously, "revert to before the bad state" stops being well-defined — there may be no clean point in a tangled, concurrent history that excludes the damage without also discarding large amounts of legitimate work, and even where such a point exists, bad state has very likely already propagated outward to users before anyone notices, so reverting the repository does not undo the harm. Second, the audit log that Recovery depends on for forensics becomes, at this volume, an attack surface in its own right: sheer log volume makes manual reconstruction infeasible, and an adversary capable of generating that volume can also attempt to poison it — burying the signal of what actually happened inside enough noise that timely recovery is no longer possible even in principle. In the scenario, this is the final compounding step: by the time investigators try to reconstruct what the agent population did, the very record they would use to do so has been degraded by the same flood that defeated the first three layers.

Run end to end, the scenario shows something the four layers cannot show in isolation: a single coordinated population of adversarial agents does not need to defeat any one safeguard outright. It needs only to push enough simultaneous load that Prevention degrades into a rubber stamp, Detection's blind spots get mapped and used, Containment's removed gate widens the blast radius, and Recovery's audit trail becomes too large and too contaminated to act on in time — four independent-looking failures that are in fact one failure, propagating through a system whose layers all quietly depended on the same human bandwidth.

This produces a perverse inversion worth stating plainly. As a system moves from one user to a population of thousands or millions, the stakes of failure rise — health-adjacent harms, financial harms, harms compounding silently across many people before anyone notices, not just the predictable misjudgment of a single founder reviewing their own project. By any reasonable account, the assurance applied to the system should rise to match. But the safeguard that provided the most assurance in the single-user case — direct, attentive human judgment, applied to every action before it takes effect — is exactly the one that becomes economically and practically unaffordable per action at the volumes where the stakes are highest. Severity climbs as the keystone becomes impossible to keep in place.
